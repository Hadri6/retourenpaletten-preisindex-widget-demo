Reverse Logistics in Germany: Where Amazon Returns Actually Go

Every day, parcels travel back through Germany's delivery networks: a jacket that did not fit, a blender with a missing part, a gift that was never wanted. For consumers, a return is usually the end of a transaction. For the logistics system, it is the beginning of a second journey - through what the industry calls reverse logistics. Understanding that journey explains a lot about how modern e-commerce works, and where the goods you see in discount channels and second-hand shops actually come from.

The path of a returned parcel

When a customer initiates a return, the item is picked up or dropped off, scanned back into the carrier network, and routed to a returns processing center. There it is identified, assessed, and sorted. A small share of returned items can go back into primary sales as new stock. Much of it cannot: packaging may be damaged, accessories may be missing, or the cost of individual inspection, reprocessing, and re-listing simply exceeds the value of the item at retail pace. For large marketplaces like Amazon, the economics of handling millions of individual returns item by item do not add up. Rather than reprocessing each unit, the system aggregates: sellable and condition-mixed goods are grouped onto pallets and liquidated in bulk, moving the handling effort to specialized buyers downstream.

Liquidation channels: pallets and truckloads

This is where the B2B liquidation market begins. Returned goods leave the platform economy as palletized lots - mixed pallets, category pallets, or larger volumes up to full truckloads and containers. Specialized wholesalers purchase these lots and resell them to commercial buyers. One example is RetourenDirekt, the B2B brand of Auctum GmbH, headquartered in Schorfheide near Berlin, which trades unopened Amazon return pallets and surplus stock from a single pallet up to container quantities, with its own warehouse operations and EU-wide delivery.

The buyers of these pallets are a mixed crowd: brick-and-mortar discounters, online resellers, flea market traders, refurbishing operations, and export businesses. Their common denominator is the willingness and capability to do what the original seller declined to do - inspect, sort, repair, repack, and resell at unit level. The margin in this business comes from that labor and from information: knowing what a pallet is likely to contain and what its contents will fetch in secondary channels.

Why buyers take on the risk

Buying unsorted returns means accepting uncertainty about condition and composition. Buyers compensate through conservative calculation, volume purchasing at tiered prices, and channel specialization - a trader focused on small appliances prices and sorts differently than one selling apparel. Over time, experienced buyers build internal benchmarks from their own purchase history. The result is a functioning secondary market that extends the life of goods which would otherwise head to disposal far earlier, which also matters from a resource-efficiency perspective.

Transparency and open data as a trend

Historically, this market has been opaque: prices quoted on request, comparisons dependent on personal networks, and little public data on what pallets actually cost. That is starting to change. Some market participants now publish structured price data openly. The same wholesaler mentioned above, for instance, publishes an open price index for return pallets under a Creative Commons license, including segment-level medians, quartiles, and sample sizes, with downloadable data files and a documented methodology.

Whether this transparency spreads remains to be seen, but the direction is notable. Open data shifts the market from pure relationship-based pricing toward reference-based negotiation, and it lowers the entry barrier for smaller buyers who lack years of internal benchmarks. For an industry that lives on assessing uncertainty, better information does not remove the risk - it just distributes it more evenly. And that, in the end, is what mature markets do.
